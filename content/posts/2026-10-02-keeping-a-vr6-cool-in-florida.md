A VR6 sounds incredible and runs warm. Pack six cylinders into a narrow V under a compact hood, add a Florida afternoon in traffic, and the cooling system earns its keep. Most VR6 overheating stories trace back to a few aging parts, and almost all of them are cheaper to replace early than after a roadside steam show.

Here's what I check, and what the older dub guys keep telling me.

## Why the VR6 is sensitive to heat

The engine is compact for six cylinders, and on older cars a lot of the cooling system is plastic that has been heat-cycled for twenty-plus years. Plastic gets brittle. Brittle parts crack on a hot day, usually when you're farthest from home.

## The usual suspects

- **Thermostat housing and coolant flanges.** Plastic fittings on many VR6 cars are known to crack or seep. Look for dried coolant crust around them.
- **Water pump.** Some pumps used plastic impellers. A weak pump means temps climb under load.
- **Radiator fans and fan control.** If the fans don't kick on when the car is hot and idling, find out why before summer.
- **Coolant temp sensor.** A failing sensor can give bad readings to the gauge or the engine computer.
- **Hoses and the expansion tank cap.** Cheap parts, big consequences.

Use the coolant type your car specifies and don't mix types. If you're unsure, ask a VW specialist.

## Watch your gauges

The factory temp gauge is buffered on many VWs, so it can sit in the middle until things are already wrong. A lot of owners read live coolant temps with a scan tool or add a gauge. Knowing your normal number makes a problem obvious.

## Habits that help in the heat

- Let it warm up gently before you get on it.
- Watch temps in long traffic and on the bridges around Tampa Bay.
- After a hard drive, let it idle a minute before shutting it off.
- Keep oil changes on schedule. Heat is hard on oil, and a VR6 works it.

> This is general owner advice, not a repair manual. For anything beyond basic checks, use your service manual or a mechanic who knows VR6s.
